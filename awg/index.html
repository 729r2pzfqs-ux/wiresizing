About the AWG System

American Wire Gauge (AWG) is the standard wire sizing system used in North America. Defined by ASTM B258, the gauge number is inversely proportional to wire diameter — smaller numbers mean thicker wire. Every 3 gauges roughly doubles (or halves) the cross-sectional area and resistance, and every 6 gauges roughly doubles the diameter.

The diameter of any AWG size is calculated from the formula: d = 0.005 × 92(36-n)/39 inches, where n is the gauge number.

Ampacity values shown are from NEC Table 310.16 for copper conductors with 75°C insulation, not more than 3 current-carrying conductors in raceway, at 30°C ambient temperature.

* NEC 240.4(D) limits overcurrent protection to 15A for 14 AWG, 20A for 12 AWG and 30A for 10 AWG copper, regardless of the table ampacity. † 16 and 18 AWG appear in Table 310.16 only in the 90°C column (18A and 14A) and are not permitted for general branch circuits.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most common AWG wire size for household wiring?

The most common sizes in residential wiring are 14 AWG (15A lighting circuits), 12 AWG (20A outlet circuits), and 10 AWG (30A circuits for dryers and water heaters). Larger 8 AWG and 6 AWG are used for ranges and EV chargers.

What AWG is equivalent to 2.5 mm²?

2.5 mm² (IEC 60228) falls between 14 AWG (2.08 mm²) and 12 AWG (3.31 mm²); 13 AWG (2.62 mm²) is closest but is not a standard building-wire size. When substituting for current-carrying capacity, round up to 12 AWG. 2.5 mm² is the standard socket-circuit size in the UK, Europe and Australia.

Does a higher AWG number mean bigger or smaller wire?

Higher AWG numbers mean SMALLER wire. 14 AWG is thinner than 10 AWG. The AWG system works backwards from how most people expect: 4/0 (0000) AWG is the largest common size, while 40 AWG is extremely thin wire used in electronics.
