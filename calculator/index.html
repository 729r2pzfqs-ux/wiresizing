NEC 240.4(D) — the small-conductor rule

NEC Table 310.16 lists a 75°C ampacity for every conductor, but for the smallest building-wire sizes NEC 240.4(D) overrides it with a lower hard limit on the overcurrent device. This is the single most common source of wrong answers in wire-size calculators: 12 AWG copper appears in Table 310.16 at 25 A, but you may never put it on a breaker larger than 20 A. This calculator applies the 240.4(D) cap.

Above 10 AWG the rule no longer applies and Table 310.16 governs directly. NEC 240.4(E) and 240.4(G) list the specific exceptions — tap conductors, motor circuits, welders, and similar loads sized under their own articles.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I use the wire size calculator?

Enter your circuit amperage, voltage (120V, 208V, 230V, 240V, 277V or 480V; single- or three-phase), wire run distance in feet, and conductor material (copper or aluminum). The calculator returns the minimum safe AWG gauge and the voltage drop percentage for that run.

What voltage drop is acceptable?

NEC recommends no more than 3% voltage drop on a branch circuit and 5% total including feeder. For sensitive equipment, keep it under 2%. For long runs, you may need to upsize the wire gauge to stay within these limits.

Should I use copper or aluminum wire?

Copper is standard for branch circuits (outlets, lighting, appliances) because it is more conductive and easier to work with. Aluminum is common for large feeder runs and service entrance where cost savings matter — you need a wire 2 sizes larger than copper for the same ampacity.
